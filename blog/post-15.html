Talking With Your Child About Feelings

February 14, 2022

Big feelings come in small packages. A dropped cracker can bring on a flood of tears, and a missed turn on the slide can feel like the end of the world. Helping children name and handle emotions is one of the most important things we do, at school and at home.

Name it to tame it

When a child is upset, try putting words to what you see: "You look frustrated. The tower keeps falling down." Naming a feeling helps children understand what is happening inside them, and it shows that their feelings make sense.

All feelings are okay

We teach children that every feeling is allowed, even anger and sadness. What we guide is the behavior. "You can feel mad, but I won't let you hit. You can stomp your feet or squeeze this pillow."

Use books and play

Picture books are a wonderful way to talk about emotions without pressure. Pause and ask, "How do you think she feels? What would you do?" Pretend play with stuffed animals works well too.

Model it yourself

Children learn by watching. Saying "I'm feeling a little stressed, so I'm going to take three deep breaths" teaches more than any lecture could.

What it looks like in our classrooms

Many of our classrooms have a calm corner with soft pillows, feelings cards, and simple breathing pictures. Children can visit when they need a moment. Teachers check in and help them talk through what happened once they are ready.

This Valentine's Day, as we celebrate love and friendship, we are reminded that kindness starts with understanding ourselves. When children learn to recognize their own feelings, they become better at noticing the feelings of others. That is the beginning of empathy, and it is a gift that lasts a lifetime.
